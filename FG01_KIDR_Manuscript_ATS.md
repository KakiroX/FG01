# Abstract

**Background.** Objects between roughly 1 and 10 cm in characteristic length fall below the reliable detection and cataloging threshold of ground-based surveillance networks in low Earth orbit, leaving this population outside the perception layer that space traffic management and conjunction assessment depend on, despite retaining enough mass and closing velocity (7–15 km/s) to catastrophically damage an operational spacecraft. Targeted momentum-transfer remediation — an electromagnetically launched, briefly-dispersing metal-grain cloud delivering a deorbiting kick to one terminally-tracked object at a time — has been proposed as a strategy that requires only short-arc terminal perception rather than catalog-grade tracking or rendezvous.

**Purpose.** An earlier iteration of this concept, sized to force a target's immediate reentry, was found not viable on four independent grounds (a structural conflict between engineering safety margin and catastrophic-fragmentation risk, collapse of terminal-guidance hit probability at realistic control latencies, net debris increase, and cost). This study reports a redesigned accelerated-reentry variant — sized to shift the target's orbit by a fixed decrement and let existing atmospheric drag complete the deorbit — under the same falsification-first methodology, extended with a resolved grain-swarm interaction model, a revised engagement-economics analysis, and a from-first-principles rebuild of the comparison against ground- and space-based laser ablation.

# 1. Introduction

Space traffic management in low Earth orbit (LEO) — conjunction assessment, collision-avoidance maneuver planning, and the catalog infrastructure both depend on — is built around the assumption that a hazard object can first be detected, tracked, and maintained in a catalog. That assumption fails for objects between roughly 1 and 10 cm in characteristic length: this population sits below the reliable detection threshold of ground-based surveillance networks in LEO, so of the estimated 1.2 million objects of this size currently in orbit, only on the order of 3% are individually cataloged [1,2]. These objects are consequently invisible to the traffic-deconfliction systems that keep operational spacecraft apart from known hazards, while retaining enough mass and closing velocity — typically 7–15 km/s in random encounters — to catastrophically damage or destroy a spacecraft they are never flagged as approaching. This is not a gap in debris removal capability so much as a gap in the traffic-safety picture itself: an entire size class of collision hazard exists outside the perception layer that space traffic management is built on.

Active debris removal (ADR) capability now under demonstration does not close this gap, because it inherits the same tracked-target assumption from the opposite direction. JAXA's Commercial Removal of Debris Demonstration (CRD2) has characterized and approached a large, catalogued upper stage [3], and ESA's planned ClearSpace-1 mission targets a similarly large, tracked adapter for robotic-arm capture [4]; both are rendezvous operations against objects many orders of magnitude more massive than the population addressed here, and both require the same prior detection-and-cataloging step that the sub-10 cm population does not receive. A traffic-safety strategy aimed specifically at closing this blind spot therefore cannot be a rendezvous strategy: it must operate on objects that are, at best, perceived and tracked only terminally, immediately before an engagement, rather than catalogued in advance.

Two prior concepts have been proposed on this basis. Ganguli et al. proposed an untargeted, persistent drag-enhancement layer — tens of tonnes of micron-scale tungsten dust injected into a debris-populated shell to raise ambient drag on everything passing through it, accelerating decay of the entire untracked population without perceiving or targeting any individual object [5]. This was not adopted operationally, for reasons directly relevant to any successor strategy: indiscriminate drag enhancement degrades the orbits of operational spacecraft sharing the same shell exactly as it does the debris; the injected dust is itself a new, if short-lived, object population; and a persistent metallic layer carries documented objections from ground-based astronomy. The second prior concept, stand-off laser ablation, replaces persistent bulk drag with per-object recoil impulse from vaporized surface material, requiring the target to be perceived and tracked only over the terminal engagement window. Ground-based laser ablation is the only concept in this class with a published cost benchmark specifically claimed to reach the sub-10 cm population ($100–500/kg, theoretical) [6], and it recurs throughout this study as the primary comparator for that reason; a space-based variant of the same mechanism is examined later in this study as well.

FG01 (referred to in earlier internal documents by the working name KIDR) is evaluated here as a third strategy for the same traffic-safety gap: a targeted momentum-transfer kick, delivered from a dedicated orbital platform via an electromagnetically launched, briefly-dispersing cloud of metal grains, sized to alter one object's trajectory per engagement rather than to blanket a shell. Terminal perception and short-arc tracking of the target — not catalog-grade orbit determination — is the information requirement this strategy places on the traffic-safety architecture, and it is examined as such (Section 2). Rhenium was the concept's originally proposed projectile material, on the premise that its density (21.01 g/cm³, the fourth-highest among stable elements) would improve momentum-transfer efficiency; this premise is shown in Section 2 not to survive first-principles examination and is reported as a negative finding rather than carried forward silently.

An earlier iteration of this same analysis evaluated a design target in which the delivered impulse was sized to force the target's immediate reentry, and found that design not viable on four independent grounds evaluated jointly: the engineering safety margin needed to reliably deliver sufficient impulse was itself, as an algebraic consequence of momentum balance, sufficient to push the impact energy into the catastrophic-fragmentation regime of the NASA Standard Breakup Model [7]; a terminal-guidance miss-distance model driven by the target's closing velocity collapsed hit probability to effective zero at control latencies achievable with current actuator technology; the resulting fragmentation produced a net increase in the very population this strategy is meant to reduce; and gross engagement cost exceeded every competing method considered. That result is retained in full here, not set aside, consistent with this study's standing commitment to report unfavorable findings with the same prominence as favorable ones.

Rather than abandoning the concept on that basis, this study's central methodological move is to ask whether the immediate-reentry design target — as opposed to targeted momentum-transfer remediation in general — was responsible for those specific failures. Sizing the delivered impulse instead to shift the target's orbit by a fixed decrement (of order 200 km), and allowing existing atmospheric drag to complete the deorbit over the following months to years, is shown in Section 2 to directly lift the margin-versus-fragmentation conflict, because both the required impulse and the resulting impact energy scale down together under this redesign. The terminal-guidance collapse is shown separately to follow from a different, previously unexamined assumption — that the target sweeps across the aim point at its full closing velocity — which the redesign's own engagement geometry replaces with a much slower relative sweep rate; correcting this assumption, rather than the sensing or actuator hardware, is what resolves the second failure.

This study reports the resulting reassessment in full, extended in three respects directly relevant to a traffic-safety evaluation.
