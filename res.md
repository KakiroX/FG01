Results
Motion Test
Adhesion of restored EMG electrodes was assessed by a standardized motion test over five use-restoration cycles. In Cycle 1, PVP-treated electrodes demonstrated the highest adhesion (229.6 ± 8.8 movements), significantly exceeding HEC (146.2 ± 7.2), A solution (129.0 ± 4.4), Water (47.6 ± 4.2), and Dirty controls (33.4 ± 3.8). ANOVA confirmed significant differences between groups (F = 881.8, p < 0.001).
Over successive cycles, all groups exhibited progressive degradation. By Cycle 5, PVP retained 84.9% of initial adhesion (195.0 ± 5.7 movements), compared to 75.8% for HEC (110.8 ± 4.3), 76.6% for A solution (98.8 ± 2.7), 44.5% for Water (21.2 ± 3.9), and 39.5% for Dirty (13.2 ± 4.3). Paired t-tests confirmed significant degradation for all groups (p < 0.001).
The degradation rate was highest for HEC (8.8 movements per cycle) and PVP (8.6), moderate for A solution (7.6), and lowest for Water (6.6) and Dirty (5.0). However, because PVP started from a substantially higher baseline, its absolute performance remained superior throughout all cycles.
Load Test
Load-test results paralleled motion-test findings. In Cycle 1, PVP sustained 146.0 ± 5.5 g, followed by HEC (116.0 ± 11.4 g), A solution (88.0 ± 8.4 g), Water (40.0 ± 7.1 g), and Dirty (20.0 ± 7.1 g). ANOVA again revealed significant inter-group differences (F = 206.4, p < 0.001).
By Cycle 5, PVP retained 60.3% of initial load capacity (88.0 ± 13.0 g). Unexpectedly, A solution showed the highest relative retention (68.2%, 60.0 ± 15.8 g), while HEC dropped to 56.9% (66.0 ± 13.4 g). Water and Dirty exhibited poor retention (60.0% and 30.0%, respectively), with Dirty approaching complete failure (6.0 ± 5.5 g).
Comparative Analysis
PVP consistently outperformed HEC in absolute terms across both tests. In the motion test, PVP maintained a 75–80 movement advantage over HEC throughout all cycles. In the load test, this advantage narrowed from 30 g in Cycle 1 to 22 g in Cycle 5, suggesting that HEC's mechanical strength degrades more rapidly under sustained loading.
A solution performed surprisingly well in the load test, exceeding HEC in relative retention. This may indicate that A solution's cleaning mechanism preserves the underlying gel structure better than HEC's film-forming approach, though HEC's initial coating provides stronger surface adhesion.
Water rinse and Dirty controls confirmed that simple cleaning or no treatment is insufficient for electrode refurbishment. Water performed marginally better than Dirty in the motion test but equivalently in the load test, suggesting that moisture alone temporarily restores surface tack without reinforcing structural integrity.
Statistical Significance
All pairwise comparisons between Cycle 1 and Cycle 5 were statistically significant (p < 0.05), confirming that repeated use-restoration cycles inevitably degrade electrode adhesion. The independent t-test between PVP and HEC in Cycle 1 was highly significant (t = 16.4, p < 0.001), establishing PVP's superior baseline performance.
Discussion (краткая версия)
The present study demonstrates that polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) is a more effective restoration agent than hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC) for reusable EMG electrodes. PVP's superior performance likely stems from its hydrophilic nature and ability to form hydrogen-bonded interpolymer complexes with the electrode's hydrogel matrix, restoring both surface tack and structural cohesion.
HEC's poorer showing was unexpected given its established use in biomedical adhesives. Possible explanations include: (1) suboptimal concentration in our formulation, (2) excessive film rigidity reducing conformal contact with skin, or (3) incomplete rehydration of the underlying gel layer. Further optimization of HEC molecular weight and concentration may improve outcomes.
The progressive degradation across all groups highlights a fundamental limitation of electrode refurbishment: no restoration protocol fully compensates for cumulative mechanical and chemical wear. Nevertheless, PVP's 85% retention in motion tests and 60% in load tests suggests that 3–4 reuse cycles are feasible before performance falls below clinically acceptable thresholds.
